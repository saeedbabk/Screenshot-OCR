Pull on Earth

01

Tides — the ocean's twice-daily breath

Gravity weakens with distance, so the Moon pulls the near side of Earth harder than the planet's centre, and the centre harder than the far side. This difference in pull — the tidal force — stretches the oceans into two bulges: one facing the Moon, one directly opposite. As Earth rotates beneath these bulges, every coastline sweeps through roughly two high tides and two low tides a day.

  • ~12h 25m between successive high tides
  • 2 bulges near side & far side
  • ~0.5 m open-ocean tidal range, far larger at coasts
02

Spring & neap — when the Sun joins in

The Sun raises tides too, at about 46% the strength of the Moon's. At new and full moon the two line up and their pulls add: spring tides, with the highest highs and lowest lows. At the quarter moons they pull at right angles and partly cancel: gentler neap tides. Drag the Sun alignment slider in the model to swing between the two.

03

The lengthening day

The tidal bulges don't sit perfectly under the Moon — Earth's fast rotation drags them slightly ahead. Their gravity tugs back on the spinning planet like a brake, a process called tidal friction. Earth's rotation is slowing by about 1.7 milliseconds per century. In the age of the dinosaurs a day was barely 23 hours; growth bands in fossil corals show years that once held over 400 days.

  • +1.7 ms added to the day each century
  • ~23 h day length in the late Cretaceous
  • ~620 Mya the day was only ~21.9 hours
04

The Moon is drifting away

Conservation of angular momentum balances the books: the spin Earth loses, the Moon gains as orbital energy. So it climbs into a wider orbit, retreating about 3.8 cm a year — roughly the rate your fingernails grow. Laser ranging off reflectors left by the Apollo astronauts measures this drift directly. Far in the future, total solar eclipses will end as the Moon appears too small to cover the Sun.

  • 3.8 cm/yr recession, measured by laser
  • 384,400 km mean distance today
  • Apollo 11/14/15 retroreflectors still in use
05

Tidal locking — one face, forever

The same friction that brakes Earth long ago stopped the Moon's spin relative to us. Earth raised tides in the Moon's rock until its rotation matched its orbit — tidal locking. That's why we only ever see one hemisphere; the "far side" stayed hidden until 1959.

06

A steady tilt, stable seasons

Earth's axis tilts about 23.4°, and the Moon's mass acts as a stabiliser, damping how far that tilt can wander. Without it, gravitational nudges from the other planets could swing the tilt chaotically over millions of years — wild swings in climate. The Moon helps keep our seasons recognisable across the ages.
